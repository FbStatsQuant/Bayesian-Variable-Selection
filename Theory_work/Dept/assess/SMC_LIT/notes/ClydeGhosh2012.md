## 2. Model and prior
- Linear regression variable selection, model M_gamma with gamma in {0,1}^p (p. 981). Simulation (p. 985): n = 50, p = 20, design following Nott & Kohn (2005): first 15 columns as in Nott-Kohn, columns 16-19 independent N(0,1) and column 20 correlated 0.99 with column 19 (as I read p. 985); y ~ N(alpha 1 + X beta, 2.5^2 I) with alpha = 4; Zellner g-prior with g = n for model-specific parameters (closed-form marginal likelihood); model prior p(M_gamma) = 1/2^p (uniform). (I did not transcribe beta, which is printed on p. 985.) Model space: all 2^20 subsets; the exact posterior is available by enumeration, which is how "Truth" in Table 1 is computed.

## 3. Sampler design
- Not an SMC paper. Sampler used for the simulation: Metropolis-Hastings on gamma with add/delete and random-swap proposals (Clyde et al 2011), 10,000 iterations, first 1000 discarded, 100 replicates from random starting points (p. 985). The paper is about ESTIMATORS from a sampled set of models, not about the sampler.
- Estimators compared (p. 985-986), all from the same computational budget of 10,000 iterations:
  - "Monte Carlo"/empirical frequency p_hat^MC (and Delta_hat^MC = sum Delta(M) p_hat^MC);
  - "Renormalised": R = a_bar/b_bar in (4) with a_gamma = Delta(M_gamma) p(Y|M_gamma) p(M_gamma) and b_gamma = p(Y|M_gamma) p(M_gamma), sums over the set S_T of UNIQUE sampled models, i.e. exactly Delta_hat = sum_{S_T} Delta(M) p(Y|M)p(M) / sum_{S_T} p(Y|M)p(M). With Delta = 1{gamma_j = 1} this is precisely our pip_T structure, with S_T = all unique models visited by the chain (not a top-K list);
  - "Horvitz-Thompson (ratio)": same ratio but each model's term divided by pi^S(M_gamma), its probability of being in the sample set (Proposition 3). First 8000 post-burn-in draws, thinned by 8 (T* = 1000 approximately independent draws), plus a second independent 1000-iteration chain to determine the set A used in C_hat (p. 985).
- Inclusion-probability model for HT (p. 985): exact pi^S under MCMC would need the 2^p x 2^p transition matrix (p. 984), so they use pi^S(M_gamma) = 1 - {1 - p(M_gamma | Y)}^{T*} = 1 - {1 - C p(Y|M)p(M)}^{T*}, with C unknown and estimated by C_hat = [sum_{t=1}^{T*} I(M^(t) in A) / T*] x [1 / sum_{gamma in A} p(Y|M_gamma) p(M_gamma)], A = unique models from the independent second chain. (My remark: C_hat is a "frequency-calibrated" normaliser: the visit fraction of A divided by the unnormalised mass of A; it is itself not a renormalised-over-visited-models quantity.)
- Largest p: 20 (n = 50). No large-p experiments. They state that larger model spaces would make renormalised bias and variability "much larger than empirical estimators, as seen in the technical report by García-Donato and Martínez-Beneito" (p. 987), which is not their own result.

## 4. What they prove
All statements are for a finite state space and a fixed data set; the randomness is the sampling of models.
- Prop. 1 (p. 982-983). Aperiodic, positive recurrent chain on Gamma with initial law alpha, transition P(t), stationary law pi = p(.|Y). Then E(p_hat^MC)' = T^{-1} alpha' sum_{t=1}^T P(t). Type (i): finite-T bias of empirical frequencies depends on alpha (asymptotically unbiased; ergodic CLT cited, p. 983). Standard.
- Prop. 2 (p. 983-984) [the "bias of renormalised" result]. Let Delta = Delta_num / Delta_den = sum Delta(M) p(Y|M)p(M) / sum p(Y|M)p(M), and R = a_bar / b_bar as in (4) with rho := E(a_bar)/E(b_bar) = mu_a / mu_b. Then
  E(R) - Delta = rho - Delta - cov(R, b_bar) / mu_b,
  and the absolute relative bias satisfies |E(R) - Delta| / sigma_R <= |Delta - rho| / sigma_R + sigma_b_bar / |mu_b|. (5)
  Type (iii)-like but for an estimator, a generalisation of Hartley-Ross (1954). It is an exact identity/inequality, not a rate. The second term is the coefficient of variation of the denominator. The first term |rho - Delta| is zero iff the expected numerator and denominator are unbiased.
- Prop. 3 (p. 984). Let pi^S(M_gamma) = probability that M_gamma is in the set of unique models S_T. With a_gamma^HT = Delta(M) p(Y|M)p(M) / pi^S(M), b^HT analogously, the Horvitz-Thompson totals a_bar^HT, b_bar^HT are unbiased for Delta_num, Delta_den; hence rho = Delta and the ratio R^HT is approximately unbiased (first-order Taylor linearisation), with approximate MSE E(R^HT - Delta)^2 ~ var(a_bar^HT - Delta b_bar^HT) / Delta_den^2 (6). David & Sukhatme (1974) are cited for rigour of the ratio approximations (p. 984); no further rigour is given.
- Consequence stated in text (p. 984): "the renormalized estimator is a ratio of Horvitz-Thompson estimators and approximately unbiased only in the case of simple random sampling with or without replacement where each model has an equal probability of being selected ... in all other cases rho != Delta." (The "only" is argued from Prop. 2/3; no counterexample bound or rate is given.)
- What the "we prove ... generally leads to bias that may dominate mean squared error" in the Summary amounts to: Prop. 2 (a bias identity and bound) plus the observation after Prop. 3. Nothing proves that the bias does NOT vanish as T grows; the paper only says (p. 986, simulation) that with chains 10 times longer the absolute relative bias of the renormalised estimator "remains the same or doubles in 50% of the cases" and "decreases at a slower rate than the standard deviation". In the Discussion (p. 987) they also say renormalised estimators "are consistent" (they converge to the exact posterior quantity under enumeration of the model space) but biased in finite samples. No bound on the bias in terms of unvisited posterior mass is given.
- No result for SMC or any sampler other than MCMC; no result for a "top-K list" estimator.

### Reading of Prop. 2 for our pip_T (my rewriting, not in the paper)
For Delta(M) = 1{gamma_j = 1} and sampling law pi^S, rho = E_pi[Delta pi^S] / E_pi[pi^S], so rho - Delta = Cov_pi(Delta, pi^S) / E_pi[pi^S]. The (first-order) bias of the renormalised estimator is the posterior covariance between "j is included" and "the model is visited". It is zero when pi^S is constant on the support (equal-probability sampling). If pi^S is close to 1 on every model carrying non-negligible posterior mass (the list carries almost all the mass), the covariance is small. Table 1 is the case where pi^S is strongly increasing in posterior probability and many models of moderate mass are unvisited.

## Evidence in the simulation (p. 985-987, Table 1, p. 986)
- Table 1 (p = 20, n = 50, 100 replicates; entries x 100 for inclusion probabilities, x 10^4 for the model-indicator row): bias, s.e. (across replicates), RMSE.
  - Renormalised inclusion probabilities: bias about -5 (i.e. -0.05) for variables with true PIP 0.13 to 0.19 (e.g. gamma_6 truth 0.13 bias -5.04, RMSE 5.06; gamma_5 truth 0.19 bias -4.69), -3.33 (truth 0.27) and -2.33 (truth 0.45); positive for truth > 0.5 (gamma_19 truth 0.72 bias +2.00; gamma_11 truth 0.81 bias +4.74); small for truth about 1. s.e. only about 0.5-1.3 (x 0.01) for the variables with PIP below 1. Text (p. 986): inclusion probabilities larger than 0.5 are overestimated and those smaller than 0.5 underestimated (a sharpening towards 0/1), "errors for inclusion probabilities often around 5%", "orders of magnitude" larger bias than empirical or HT.
  - Empirical (MC) and HT: bias negligible; s.e. of about 1.5-4.4 (x 0.01) for PIPs below 1. HT has the smallest MSE for about 80% of the inclusion probabilities not equal to 1 and is "substantially more efficient than Monte Carlo for inclusion probabilities near 0.5" (e.g. gamma_19 RMSE 3.24 vs 4.41; gamma_20 3.28 vs 4.51; for small PIPs the two are almost equal, e.g. 1.48 vs 1.57). For model probabilities (the 2^20 vector) HT has the smallest MSE (aggregate RMSE 0.12 vs 0.30 empirical vs 0.45 renormalised, x 10^4).
  - Important for presentation: "As the sampler visits the same top models most of the 100 replicates, the renormalized estimators exhibit low variability and their mean squared error is dominated by bias" (p. 986). Run-to-run agreement of a renormalised estimator is not evidence of accuracy.
  - Bound (5) in the simulation: coefficient of variation of the normalising constant estimate 0.014 (renormalised) and 0.092 (HT); for the renormalised estimator the extra term |rho - Delta| / sigma_R ranged from 1.60 to 11.24 for inclusion probabilities not equal to 1 (p. 986). The paper does not report the posterior mass captured by S_T (so we cannot translate their bias into 1 - mass).
- Their stated alternatives (p. 985-987): ratio Horvitz-Thompson estimators (computed via thinned chain + second chain); more generally "regression estimators or calibration estimators" from the survey-sampling literature (Hesterberg 1995; Theberge 1999) and model-based methods as possible improvements (p. 987, flagged as future work, not done). The Rao-Blackwell improvement given the minimal sufficient statistic (unordered distinct models) is noted as hard to compute and "rarely used" (p. 983).

- Theory vs ours: Their Props 1-3 do not overlap with Theorems 1-6 or P1-P5. They bear on how pip_T must be described. (1) Under their sampling-theory framework pip_T is generally biased when the list is not (almost) all of the posterior mass; our Cor 6L is the opposite regime (list carries most mass, as under Thm 1 concentration) and is consistent with Prop. 2 rather than contradicted by it, but it must not be presented as "unbiased" or as a posterior inclusion probability in general. (2) Elementary deterministic bound that Cor 6L-type statements should be compared with (my own calculation, not from the paper; check before use): if m = pi(T | y) is the posterior mass of the list, then |pip_T(j) - PIP_j| <= 1 - m for every j (write a = pi(S in T, S contains j), b = pi(S not in T, S contains j): pip_T - PIP = a(1-m)/m - b, with a <= m and b <= 1-m). So pip_T is a PIP estimator exactly to the extent that 1 - m is small. m can be estimated by Z_T / Z with Z from the unbiased SMC evidence estimate, if on a common scale (to be checked in our code). Their simulation shows bias of order 0.05 at p = 20 where the visited set is a small part of 2^20 models (the captured mass is not reported, so this is my inference that it is well below 1).
- HT applicability: Prop. 3 needs inclusion probabilities pi^S(S) that are known or estimable; our list is the best-scoring 40p+1 models among all scored over several islands, an order-statistic selection, not a draw with a computable pi^S. So their HT recipe is not directly available for pip_T (my inference). Its natural analogue for us is the RB-SMC PIP (empirical type, no renormalisation).

## 6. Verdict
- Novelty risk to us: NONE for the method and theorems. It is a MUST-CITE for how we present pip_T: it shows that renormalising over the visited models is biased for finite samples (Prop. 2; Table 1, about 5% error at p = 20) and that run-to-run stability hides that bias.
- What we must do in the paper: call pip_T "list-renormalised PIP", state that it is a deterministic function of the returned list, report the list mass (estimated) next to it, quote the bound |pip_T - PIP| <= 1 - m (after verification), and present Cor 6L as a result about the regime where the list carries most mass; use the RB-SMC PIP as the primary PIP estimate; do not use across-seed variability of pip_T as a measure of its accuracy.
- One-sentence differentiation: "Following Clyde and Ghosh (2012), who show that renormalisation over visited models is generically biased unless the sampled set captures essentially all posterior mass, we report the list-renormalised pip_T only together with the list mass and prove its accuracy exactly in the regime where the list carries most of the mass."
