## 1. Coverage log (arXiv v1, 22 pages)
- p. 1-3: abstract; Sec. 1 "Inferences in large model spaces": empirical (frequencies of visited models, MCMC) vs re-normalized (Bayes factors renormalised over visited models, e.g. BAS, Berger-Molina, FINCS) approaches; the authors' claim that empirical estimators are PPS (probability proportional to size) estimators, hence unbiased, and carry a measure of precision; outline.
- p. 4-5: Sec. 2 Bayesian variable selection: Gaussian linear model (1), Zellner g-prior (default g = N), Bayes factor B_{gamma 0}(g) = (1 + g SSE_gamma/SSE_0)^{-(N-1)/2} (1+g)^{(N-k_gamma-1)/2} (3); posterior Pr(M_gamma | y) = C B_{gamma0} Pr(M_gamma) with C^{-1} = sum_gamma B_{gamma0} Pr(M_gamma) (2).
- p. 5-7: Sec. 3 Search in large model spaces: M* in empirical methods (Gibbs/MCMC, Appendix A) and in renormalised methods (George-McCulloch 1997 remark; BAS, Berger-Molina SSBM, FINCS; sampling without replacement; search guided by estimated inclusion probabilities "potentially leads to biased results").

## 2. Model and prior
- Gaussian linear regression with intercept, all 2^p models (no size cap), full-rank X; Zellner g-prior with g = N (unit information) on the centred design; improper prior on (alpha, sigma); hierarchical g-priors (Liang et al, Zellner-Siow, robust, etc.) mentioned and a Gibbs sampler given in Appendix A (p. 4-5, 19). Model prior: constant Pr(M_gamma) in both examples (p. 12). Marginal likelihood: closed-form Bayes factor (3).

## 3. Sampler design
- Not an SMC paper. Methods compared (p. 12-13): "Freq" = Gibbs sampler (Appendix A) with frequency-based (empirical) estimates, 10,000 iterations, no burn-in, started at the null model; "BAS" = Bayesian adaptive sampling (Clyde et al 2010) via R package BAS with method "MCMC+BAS", update = 500 (without replacement, estimates renormalised over visited models); "SSBM" = Berger & Molina (2005) stochastic search adapted to the g-prior (renormalised). Ten independent runs of each.
- Outputs compared: inclusion probabilities, MPM (median probability model), HPM, and the "sum of Bayes factors (log10) of the 1000 most probable different models explored" as a measure of mass discovered.
- Largest p: 65 (N = 178), exact answer available only for p = 35.

## 4. What they prove
- No theorems or lemmas. The theory is a textbook sampling-theory observation (p. 9-10): if M* = {gamma^(1..n)} were i.i.d. draws with replacement from the posterior (probability proportional to size sampling with size Pr(M|y), Lohr 1999), the empirical (frequency) estimator tau_hat(a) = (1/n) sum a(M_(j)) is the Hansen-Hurwitz estimator and is unbiased for tau(a) = sum a(M) Pr(M|y) with the variance given above, which for a probability p is p(1-p)/n <= 1/(4n), and (5) is an unbiased variance estimator. They concede the draws are dependent MCMC draws, so the properties "strictly speaking" do not hold, but "our experience ... is that these properties (the unbiasedness ... and the expression for V_hat) hold quite accurately in practice"; thinning and burn-in are the standard remedies (p. 10).
- For renormalised estimators: "The properties of these estimators are in general difficult to derive" (p. 11); no bias formula, no consistency, no result on when they are good. Pointer to Clyde & Ghosh (2010) for single-model-probability bias.
- Type: (i)-like Monte Carlo unbiasedness (for i.i.d. sampling only) plus empirical evidence; nothing on consistency of selection, nothing on algorithm output.

## What they show about renormalised-over-visited-models estimators (arXiv v1)
- Numbers on Ozone35 (p. 12): sum of all Bayes factors 1.13e50; the HPM has posterior probability 0.0009; the first 1000 most probable models carry a total probability of only 0.07 (log10 sum of Bayes factors 48.92 vs log10 total 50.05); the MPM has posterior probability 23 times lower than the HPM and there are 851 models more probable than the MPM. So the posterior is very flat: the exact top-1000 list holds 7% of the mass.
- Mass discovered (p. 14): mean (sd over 10 runs) of log10 sum of Bayes factors of the 1000 best distinct visited models: Freq 48.77 (0.01), BAS 48.64 (0.05), SSBM 48.50 (0.20), versus exact 48.92. That is, the top-1000 distinct models visited hold about 5% (Freq), 4% (BAS) and 3% (SSBM) of the posterior mass (10^{lower value - 50.05}, my arithmetic, against 7% for the exact top 1000); the paper says the three "behave similarly" in finding good models and that Freq gives more stable answers.
- Inclusion probabilities (Table 2, p. 15; exact q_l vs estimates after one run of 10,000 iterations): Freq errors are small (e.g. x6x8 exact 0.560, Freq 0.564; x7x10 0.743 vs 0.756; x6^2 0.532 vs 0.524; x6x7 0.636 vs 0.634), with estimated sd (5) close to the observed sd over ten runs. BAS and SSBM (renormalised) are far off: x6x8 BAS 0.929, SSBM 0.077; x6^2 BAS 0.282, SSBM 0.98; x6x7 BAS 0.493, SSBM 1 (exact 0.636); x7x10 BAS 0.965, SSBM 0.879 (exact 0.743); x7x8 SSBM 0.975 (exact 0.349); x8x10 SSBM 0.597 (exact 0.236). Text (p. 14): "BAS and SSBM are clearly biased: for certain covariates we have to move from the point estimation more than 10 times the standard deviation to cover the exact value"; "inclusion probabilities within these methods are very influenced by the estimated HPM, leading to a bias in the direction of the HPM ... the manifestation of a search in the model space for good models guided by the inclusion probabilities". The preprint also states generally (p. 7) that guiding the search by estimated inclusion probabilities "potentially leads to biased results".
- MPM (p. 13): Freq identified the exact MPM in 10 of 10 runs; BAS and SSBM in none; HPM (p. 14): BAS 9/10, SSBM 5/10, and Freq's visited set contained the exact HPM in all ten runs.
- Ozone65 (p. 16-18, Table 3): n = 100,000 iterations, ten runs. Freq MPM almost unanimous (differs in 1 run by x1x4, q_hat = 0.497, sd 0.002); the number of different variables appearing in the MPM across the ten runs is 6 (Freq), 13 (BAS) and 20 (SSBM) (Table 3), and the text says BAS and SSBM produced 8 and 10 different models as MPM in the ten runs; the estimated MPM "mimics the estimated HPM" for BAS/SSBM. The best model seen across all 30 runs (log10 BF 50.87) was visited in four of the ten BAS runs, three Freq runs and one SSBM run (p. 17); mean (sd) log10 sum of BFs of the top-1000 distinct visited models: Freq 52.77 (0.02), BAS 52.78 (0.15), SSBM 52.78 (0.16).
- Conclusion (p. 17): "empirical estimates are in general, under the common assumptions in MCMC sampling, unbiased", with easily derived uncertainty; for the HPM both approaches behave similarly; "in the estimation of other important parameters like the inclusion probabilities, re-normalized methods can be strongly biased".
- Important qualifier: the renormalised competitors here are search algorithms (BAS, SSBM) that visit distinct models guided by estimated inclusion probabilities, and the renormalisation is over the set of visited models. They do not test the renormalisation of a good MCMC/SMC sample, nor a top-K list of exact scores, and they do not report the posterior mass of the sets used for renormalisation. Their theory (PPS/Hansen-Hurwitz) concerns frequency-based estimators, not renormalised ones.
- They do not propose a corrected renormalised estimator or a Horvitz-Thompson-type alternative (v1 stays with the empirical estimator). The Horvitz-Thompson alternative is Clyde & Ghosh (2012), who cite this preprint. (JASA version unknown on this point.)

- Theory vs ours: none overlap with Theorems 1-6 / P1-P5. Their sampling-theory unbiasedness argument is for i.i.d. draws; our Prop 5 (unbiased evidence) is of a different nature.
- What this implies for presenting pip_T. (1) The evidence at p = 35 and p = 65 shows renormalised inclusion probabilities from searches can be off by several tenths (up to about 0.6 in Table 2) in a flat posterior (top-1000 exact list has only 7% of the mass) and are biased towards the HPM neighbourhood; our list is selected by score (a top-by-score list), so it has the same tilt towards high-score models, and in flat/correlated posteriors pip_T can be misleading. (2) In their experiment the frequency-based estimator is both accurate and equipped with an easy variance estimate (5); we should present the RB-SMC PIP as our PIP estimate and pip_T as the list-restricted summary, and report the gap between them. (3) Cor 6L (list carries most mass, under the Thm 1 concentration regime) is the regime in which their bias mechanism disappears; the bias mechanism is exactly the mass outside the list (see the bound |pip_T - PIP| <= 1 - m in notes/ClydeGhosh2012.md; my calculation). (4) Both papers' evidence is for g-prior with g = N, constant model prior, n = 178, p up to 65, not our beta-binomial/ridge-g prior at p up to 10^6.

## 6. Verdict
- Novelty risk to us: NONE (they are about estimators for MCMC and search methods in model spaces up to p = 65, no SMC, no theory beyond Hansen-Hurwitz). MUST-CITE together with Clyde & Ghosh (2012) wherever pip_T is introduced and in any statement about estimators based on visited models.
- Caveat: the JASA version and its supplement were not read; before citing JASA specifics (the exact prior, the number of examples, supplement content) the JASA PDF has to be obtained through library access.
- One-sentence differentiation: "As documented by Garcia-Donato and Martinez-Beneito (2013) and proved by Clyde and Ghosh (2012), renormalising over a set of visited models can be strongly biased when that set misses posterior mass; we therefore use the Rao-Blackwellised SMC PIP as our estimator and analyse the list-renormalised pip_T only under a list-mass condition."
