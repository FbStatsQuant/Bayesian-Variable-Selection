     approach for ultrahigh dimensional regression

        Summary. We propose a Bayesian variable selection approach for ultrahigh dimensional linear
        regression based on the strategy of split and merge. The approach proposed consists of two
        stages: split the ultrahigh dimensional data set into a number of lower dimensional subsets
        and select relevant variables from each of the subsets, and aggregate the variables selected
        from each subset and then select relevant variables from the aggregated data set. Since the
        approach proposed has an embarrassingly parallel structure, it can be easily implemented in
        a parallel architecture and applied to big data problems with millions or more of explanatory
        variables. Under mild conditions, we show that the approach proposed is consistent, i.e. the
        true explanatory variables can be correctly identified by the approach as the sample size be-
        comes large. Extensive comparisons of the approach proposed have been made with penalized
        likelihood approaches, such as the lasso, elastic net, sure independence screening and iter-
        ative sure independence screening. The numerical results show that the approach proposed
        generally outperforms penalized likelihood approaches: the models selected by the approach
        tend to be more sparse and closer to the true model.

        Keywords: Bayesian variable selection; Posterior consistency; Split and merge; Stochastic
        approximation Monte Carlo sampling

   Although these penalized likelihood approaches have been successfully applied to many prob-
lems, their performance tends to deteriorate as Pn increases. To address this issue, Fan and Lv
(2008) and Fan and Song (2010) proposed the sure independence screening (SIS) approach and
an iterative version of SIS (ISIS). SIS first reduces the dimension of the problem by screening
out those predictors with low marginal utility and then refines the variable selection by using the
lasso or SCAD. The marginal utility measures the usefulness of a single predictor for predicting
the response. For linear regression, it is simply measured by the correlation between each predic-
tor and the response. To avoid missing true predictors, ISIS iteratively selects predictors from
the remaining unselected predictors. Compared with other penalized likelihood approaches,
SIS and ISIS are more efficient in computation as they work on a dimension-reduced space.
Moreover, SIS and ISIS tend to outperform other penalized likelihood approaches in variable
selection; the models selected by SIS and ISIS tend to be more sparse.

the extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) with an L0-penalty function given by

   Parallel to penalized likelihood approaches, various Bayesian approaches have been devel-
oped to tackle this problem. These approaches advance either in a sophisticated model search
algorithm, such as evolutionary stochastic search (Bottolo and Richardson, 2010), or in a prior
specification that is particularly suitable for high dimensional problems, such as the lasso prior
(Park and Casella, 2008), the horseshoe prior (Carvalho et al., 2010; Polson and Scott, 2012),
the Ising prior (Li and Zhang, 2010), the proportion of variance explanation prior (Guan and
Stephens, 2011) and the non-local prior (Johnson and Rossel, 2012; Johnson, 2012). Although
various numerical examples have demonstrated the successes of Bayesian approaches, there are

no extensive studies to show that Bayesian approaches can lead to a consistent selection of true
predictors in Pn > n problems until quite recently; see for example Kundu and Dunson (2014)
and Bondell and Reich (2012). Strawn et al. (2012) also studied the consistency of regression co-
efficient estimation in Pn > n problems. A major concern with Bayesian approaches is their high
computational demand. Since the volume of the model space increases geometrically with the
dimension Pn, the central processor unit (CPU) time for a Bayesian approach should increase
accordingly or even faster.

   In this paper, we propose Bayesian variable selection based on the strategy of the split-and-
merge (SAM) approach. The approach proposed consists of two stages.

  (a) Split the high dimensional data into a number of lower dimensional subsets and perform
       Bayesian variable selection for each subset.

  (b) Aggregate the variables that are selected from each subset and perform Bayesian variable
       selection for the aggregated data set.

Under mild conditions, we show that the approach proposed is consistent, i.e. the true model will
be identified in probability 1 as the sample size becomes large, and that the approach proposed
has favourable time complexity compared with conventional Bayesian approaches and even
penalized likelihood approaches. Henceforth, we shall call the approach proposed the SAM
approach for simplicity of description.

   Our contribution in this paper is twofold. First, we propose a computationally feasible
Bayesian approach for ultrahigh dimensional regression. Since the SAM approach has an em-
barrassingly parallel structure, i.e. it can break the task of high dimensional variable selection
into many small tasks between which there is a dependence or communication, it can be easily
implemented in a parallel architecture and applied to big data problems with millions or more
of predictors. This has been beyond the ability of conventional Bayesian approaches, as they
directly work on the full data set. Second, under mild conditions we show that the Bayesian
approach proposed can share the same asymptotics, such as sure screening and model selection
consistency, as SIS. In spirit, the SAM approach is similar to SIS and ISIS. Its first stage serves
the purpose of dimension reduction, which screens out uncorrelated predictors; and its second
stage refines the selection of predictors. However, compared with SIS and ISIS, the SAM ap-
proach can often lead to more accurate selection of true predictors. This is because it screens
uncorrelated predictors on the basis of the marginal inclusion probability, which has incor-
porated the joint information of all predictors contained in a subset. Whereas, as previously
mentioned, SIS makes use of only the marginal information of each predictor. ISIS tries to
incorporate information from other predictors, but in an indirect way.

   Finally, we note that the SAM strategy, or otherwise known as divide and conquer, has been
often used in big data analysis; see for example Mackey et al. (2012), Lin and Xi (2011) and Xi
et al. (2009). Our use of this strategy is different from others. In the SAM approach, the data split
is done in the dimension of predictors, whereas this is done in the dimension of observations
in other work. This difference necessitates the development of new theory for variable selection
under the situation that the model is misspecified because of missing some true predictors. This
will be detailed in Section 2.3.

   The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes consistency of the SAM
approach for variable selection. Section 3 summarizes the approach and discusses some imple-
mentational issues. Section 4 illustrates its performance by using two simulated examples along
with comparisons with penalized likelihood approaches. Section 5 compares the SAM approach
with penalized likelihood approaches on two real data examples. Section 6 concludes the paper
with a brief discussion.

   There are a few remarks on this iterative procedure.

  (a) Other than true predictors, how many other predictors will be selected? Generally speak-
       ing, all the predictors that are correlated with Xt will be selected, because they are correl-
       ated with y, where Xt denotes the set of all true predictors in Dn. This means that, if all
       the predictors are linearly correlated, it is futile to apply this iterative procedure, as all the
       predictors will be selected. The rationale underlying the SAM strategy lies in the belief
       that, for a big data set, there are only a small proportion of predictors that are linearly
       correlated with Xt. It is easy to show that if there are pn predictors correlated with the
       true predictor Xt, pn  Pn, then there are at most cpn predictors to be selected into Xs~ for
       some constant c.

  (b) To ensure that all true predictors in Xs are selected, a liberal way, as we proposed, is to
       repeat the selection iteratively until no predictors can be selected. In practice, it is rare to
       have the case that 0,t is 0 exactly. Hence, the iterative procedure is not always necessary,
       and one may set an upper bound for the iteration number. In this paper, we set the iteration
       number to 2.

  (c) Theorem 4 requires the prior probability ./ > 0 for all possible models. Hence, the inde-
       pendent prior ./ = s||.1 - s/|s|-|| for some s  .0, 1/ is still applicable here. When |s|
       is large, we suggest imposing an upper bound for the model size to avoid expensive compu-
       tation caused by inverting high order matrices. Then the posterior should asymptotically
       concentrate on the models with the density function minimizing the Kullback­Leibler
       divergence under the constraint of model size. In this case, although corollary 2 does not
       hold anymore, the marginal inclusion probabilities for correlated and uncorrelated pre-
       dictors are distinct and the sure screening property of the marginal inclusion probability
       criterion should still hold.

  (d) For data splitting, one extreme choice is s = 1. In this case, the SAM method will perform
       like SIS (Fan and Lv, 2008) with only the marginal utility being used in variable screening.
       However, this choice of s is not recommended, as the SAM approach tends to work better
       with a large value of s, say s 500. A large value of s allows the joint information of pre-
       dictors to be used in variable screening, and hence the SAM approach has a better chance
       of screening out the predictors that are spuriously correlated with the response than SIS.
       Since a large model is always penalized in either stage of the SAM approach, the marginal
       inclusion probabilities of the spuriously correlated predictors tend to be low. It is often
       to have the case that |corr.xi, y/| > |corr.xj, y/|, where corr.·, ·/ denotes the correlation
       function, whereas the marginal inclusion probability of xi is lower than that of xj. This
       is because xi is highly collinear with a true predictor, say xk, and xj is complementary
       to xk for the regression. This case distinguishes the SAM approach from SIS. In addi-
       tion, a large value of s will improve the accuracy of the marginal inclusion probability-
       based variable screening procedure, which, as stated before, is multiple-hypothesis test
       based.

3.2. Simulation and hyperparameter setting

Since different predictors can be highly correlated, the posterior (7) can have a rugged en-

ergy landscape for some problems. Conventional MCMC algorithms, such as the Metropolis­

Hasting algorithm, tend to become trapped in a local mode in simulations. To avoid this problem,

the stochastic approximation Monte Carlo (SAMC) algorithm (Liang et al., 2007) was used in

this paper. The SAMC algorithm belongs to the class of adaptive MCMC algorithms. How-

ever, unlike conventional adaptive MCMC algorithms, e.g. the adaptive Metropolis algorithm

(Haario et al., 2001), which adapt the proposal distribution, the SAMC algorithm adapts the

invariant distribution at each iteration. As explained in Liang et al. (2007), the self-adjusting

mechanism of the invariant distribution enables the SAMC algorithm to be immune to local

trap problems. As shown in Liang (2009), the SAMC algorithm is essentially a dynamic impor-

tance sampling algorithm, and quantities of interest can be estimated by averaging the samples

with respect to their importance weights. Refer to the on-line supplementary material for the

details of implementation of this algorithm.

Another issue related to the implementation of the SAM approach is how to set hyperpar-

ameters. The prior distribution of the SAM approach contains an important hyperparameter,

7. Discussion

In this paper, we propose a new Bayesian variable selection approach, the so-called SAM
approach, for ultrahigh dimensional linear regression. The SAM approach works in two stages:
stage 1 screens out the predictors that are uncorrelated with the response variable, and stage 2
refines the selection of predictors. Both stages work under a Bayesian framework. Compared
with the SIS approach of Fan and Lv (2008), a significant advantage of the SAM approach is
that it makes use of the joint information of multiple predictors in predictor screening, whereas
SIS makes use of only the marginal utility of each predictor. Our numerical results, on both
simulated and real data sets, show that the SAM approach can significantly outperform SIS
and ISIS, and also significantly outperforms other penalized likelihood approaches such as the
lasso and elastic net. The models that are selected by the SAM approach are more sparse and
closer to the true model.

   The SAM approach has an embarrassingly parallel structure and can be easily implemented
in a parallel architecture. Therefore, it is ready to be applied to big data problems with millions
or more predictors. This has been beyond the ability of conventional Bayesian approaches that
directly work on the full data set. At this point, the SAM approach also distinguishes from
SIS. Although SIS does penalized regression on a reduced data set, it still needs to process
all predictors altogether in the screening stage--sorting the marginal utilities of all predictors.
However, the SAM approach never needs to process all the predictors altogether. From this
point of view, it is more suitable for big data problems.

   We note that SAM is not a standard Bayesian approach, as it does not process all data al-
together and thus only provides an approximate quantification of uncertainty for each predictor
through marginal inclusion probabilities. Since the marginal inclusion probability measures the
contribution of a predictor given all other predictors in the regression, the marginal inclusion
probability of a given predictor in the subset data regression should not be less than that in the
full data regression, although the full data regression is never run in the SAM method. This
observation is meaningful particularly for the predictors that are screened out in the first stage,
for which the marginal inclusion probabilities that are obtained in the subset data regression
provide an upper bound for their marginal inclusion probabilities in the full data regression.

   Regarding how to interpret the posterior probabilities that are associated with Bayesian model
averaging in the subset data regression in comparison with their counterparts in the full data
regression, we note that their difference can be interpreted as a difference resulting from their
priors on the model space. The subset data regression is equivalent to the use of a data-dependent
prior, which sets the prior probability to 0 for any models containing predictors that have been
screened out in stage 1. Alternatively, the estimator that is obtained in the subset data regression
can be interpreted as an Occam's razor approximation (Madigan and Raftery, 1994) to its
counterpart in the full data regression, as the models containing the predictors that are screened
out in stage 1 tend to have very low posterior probabilities.

   Although the SAM approach does not work in a standard Bayesian way, it controls the model
970 Q. Song and F. Liang                                                                             Downloaded from academic.oup.com/jrsssb/article/77/5/947/7040600 by Rice University user on 08 November 2026

sparsity as in other Bayesian methods through specifying model priors, i.e. specifying a priori
selection probability n. The multiplicity is controlled by setting n  0 as Pn  . Hence, as
implied by expression (6), the prior of the SAM approach works more like an L0-penalty. This
may be a partial reason why the SAM approach produces favourable results compared with
the lasso and SIS. The latter two methods employ an L1- or SCAD penalty which both impose
nearly 0 penalty values on nearly 0 coefficients, and thus the resulting models tend to be dense.

   Regarding parallel implementations for high dimensional variable selection, we note that
Boyd et al. (2010) proposed a parallel algorithm for the lasso and group lasso by splitting
across features. Although both Boyd's algorithm and the SAM approach work on the basis of
the same strategy of feature splitting, they are fundamentally different. The SAM approach
is more on the side of statistics; it is a new statistical algorithm which has an embarrassingly
parallel structure, and new statistical theory is required for its performance. Although Boyd's
algorithm is mainly on the side of computer science, it just implements the existing methods
in a parallel way. Since Boyd's algorithm cannot be implemented in an embarrassingly parallel
way, internode communications may significantly reduce its efficiency. In addition, as discussed
in Section 6, Boyd's algorithm may have a higher time complexity than the SAM method.

   To justify the SAM approach, we establish Bayesian posterior consistency under both situ-
ations that the model is correctly specified and misspecified. We show that, when the model is
correctly specified, the marginal inclusion probability of the true predictor will converge to 1
as the sample size becomes large; and, when the model is misspecified, the marginal inclusion
probability of the predictor that is correlated with the response will converge to 1 as the sample
size becomes large. We also show the sure screening property for the predictors that are selected
on the basis of the marginal inclusion probability and that the MAP model is consistent as an
estimator of the true model.

   In this paper, the theoretical results are established under a Gaussian prior setting for the
regression coefficients. Although this prior setting has been widely used in the literature (e.g.
Jiang (2005) and Liang et al. (2013)), it is not natural from the perspective of marginal inclusion
probability evaluation as it assigns the highest density value to the null point. It is of interest
to use a non-local prior (Johnson and Rossel, 2012), which assigns a zero probability to the
null point and may lead to a better rate of convergence of the posterior distribution. A further
extension of the SAM approach to generalized linear models is also of great interest.

   Finally, we note that, in the recent literature, there is an active development of Bayesian
sparse learning for high dimensional variable selection with local shrinkage priors (see for
example Polson and Scott (2012)), such as the Laplace prior (Park and Casella, 2008) and the
horseshoe prior (Carvalho et al., 2009, 2010), which aim to produce a sparse model by analogy
with the pure shrinkage approach of something like the lasso under the Bayesian framework.
We have conducted a comparison study to compare the performance of the horseshoe prior
and the prior that is used in this paper. The latter is also called the SAM prior in the on-line
supplementary material. As shown in Fig. 1 of the supplementary material, the SAM prior
can perform much better than the horseshoe prior. The SAM prior is posterior consistent
and efficient in computation by restricting the models to be of a manageable size, whereas the
horseshoe prior involves sampling from high dimensional normal distributions, and it is unclear
whether it is posterior consistent or not under the high dimensional setting.
