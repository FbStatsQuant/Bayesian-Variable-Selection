                     Local-to-Global Bayesian Computation



                                               Abstract
          This proposal develops a Bayesian computation framework for large structured model
      spaces. The main idea is to run fast local posterior computations on small subproblems,
      merge the resulting local structures, and then correct the merged output toward the global
      posterior distribution. The project is motivated by Bayesian variable selection, where merg-
      ing local structures is combinatorially easy but posterior uncertainty is not preserved. The
      proposed framework treats local posterior samples as particles or proposals, not as the
      final posterior. Correction is achieved through importance weighting, sequential Monte
      Carlo, tempering, or Metropolis–Hastings steps targeting the full posterior. The goal is a
      methodology strong enough for a statistics journal such as JASA or JRSSB : a clear statis-
      tical principle, rigorous validity results, and convincing demonstrations in both familiar and
      challenging structured model spaces.


1    Motivation
Many Bayesian inference problems involve posterior distributions over large structured model
spaces. Examples include variable-selection models, graphical models, partitions, and symbolic
expressions. In such problems, the posterior has the general form

                               π(M, θM | y) ∝ L(y | M, θM )π(M, θM ),

where M ∈ M is a structured model object and θM denotes the continuous parameters as-
sociated with M . The model object M may be a selected variable set, a graph, or another
combinatorial structure.
    Direct Markov chain Monte Carlo over (M, θM ) may be slow for at least three reasons. First,
the space M may be enormous. Second, the posterior may be multimodal. Third, likelihood
evaluation can be expensive. A natural strategy is therefore to divide the problem into smaller
local subproblems, run fast Bayesian computations locally, and combine the local results.
    The central statistical difficulty is that naive local-to-global merging usually does not recover
the full Bayesian posterior. Even if the merge operation is simple, posterior uncertainty can be
wrong. This point is especially clear in Bayesian variable selection: local analyses can identify
variables from different blocks, and taking their union is easy, but the resulting uncertainty may
ignore cross-block dependence.
    This project studies the following principle:

local posterior computation       −→     merge local structures      −→      correct toward the global posterior.

The correction step is essential. Local posterior samples are useful computational building
blocks, but they are not the target posterior distribution.

Definition 2 (MCMC rejuvenation). An MCMC rejuvenation step at intermediate target πt is
a Markov transition kernel Kt satisfying

                                                πt Kt = πt .

It is applied after resampling to restore particle diversity without changing the target distribution.

Proposition 2 (SMC consistency, informal). Suppose π0 , . . . , πT are well-defined probability
distributions, the incremental weights are correctly computed, and each rejuvenation kernel Kt
leaves πt invariant. Under standard regularity conditions for sequential Monte Carlo samplers,
the empirical particle measure converges weakly to πT as the number of particles tends to infinity.

    This result follows from standard SMC sampler theory (Del Moral et al., 2006). The nov-
elty of the proposed project is not the generic SMC consistency theorem; the novelty is the
construction of useful local-to-global paths and merge proposals for structured Bayesian model
spaces.

3.1   Model
Consider Gaussian linear regression

The natural merge is

This merge is combinatorially easy. However, it does not preserve the full posterior distribution.
    The main reason is cross-block dependence. Suppose two predictors Xj and Xℓ are highly
correlated but assigned to different blocks. A local analysis of the block containing j may assign
high inclusion probability to j, while a local analysis of the block containing ℓ may assign high
inclusion probability to ℓ. The merged result may then suggest that both variables should be
included. The full posterior, however, may place mass on two competing models:

                                         S = {j}        or    S = {ℓ},

with little mass on S = {j, ℓ}. Thus naive merging can destroy joint posterior uncertainty.
   This example shows that variable selection is not trivial for the proposed framework. Even
when the merge operation is easy, the posterior uncertainty problem remains hard.

3.3    Proposed algorithm for variable selection
The proposed variable-selection algorithm is:

    1. Partition predictors into possibly overlapping blocks.

    2. Run local Bayesian variable-selection chains in each block.

    3. Use local posterior samples to build a global model proposal q(S).

    4. Extend S to (S, βS , σ 2 ) by sampling or approximating the conditional posterior of contin-
       uous parameters.

    5. Correct the global proposal using importance weighting, Metropolis–Hastings, or an SMC
       path ending at the full posterior.

    A useful SMC bridge is

               πλ (S, βS , σ 2 ) ∝ {L(y | S, βS , σ 2 )}λ {L(y
                                                           e | S, βS , σ 2 )}1−λ π(S, βS , σ 2 ),

where Le summarizes the local block analyses. The correction path moves from local evidence
to the full likelihood.

3.4    Scientific role of variable selection
Variable selection is the controlled test bed for the project. It is a familiar topic, easy to
simulate, and suitable for theory. Its role is to demonstrate the key statistical message:

                             easy merge ̸⇒ valid posterior uncertainty.

This application should be used to develop the core algorithm and to produce the first rigorous
results.


4     Main Methodological Questions
The project should be organized around the following questions.


                                                        4
4.1    Question 1: What is the target?
For each algorithm, the target distribution must be explicit. The strongest version targets the
true full posterior π. If exact targeting is too expensive, the approximation must be clearly
defined and the error must be studied.

4.2    Question 2: What is the merge proposal?
The merge proposal is problem-specific. In variable selection, it may be a distribution over
variable sets constructed from local inclusion probabilities. For other structured model spaces,
it may be a problem-specific distribution over globally valid configurations.

4.3    Question 3: How is posterior uncertainty corrected?
The correction layer is the core statistical contribution. Possible correction methods include:

    1. full-posterior Metropolis–Hastings correction;

    2. sequential Monte Carlo with intermediate targets;

    3. importance tempering from local to global posterior;

    4. delayed acceptance using cheap local scores followed by full likelihood correction.

4.4    Question 4: When is correction stable?
Correction is only useful when the local proposal has enough overlap with the global poste-
rior. The project should therefore study weight degeneracy, effective sample size, cross-block
dependence, and sensitivity to block construction.


5     Potential Theoretical Results
A strong journal paper needs theory beyond an algorithm. The following results are natural
targets.

5.1    Exactness under Metropolis–Hastings correction
As shown earlier, if local-to-global proposals are used inside a valid Metropolis–Hastings algo-
rithm with full posterior ratios, then the full posterior is invariant. This result is simple but
important because it separates proposal construction from posterior validity.

5.2    SMC consistency
For a well-defined sequence of intermediate targets ending at the full posterior, standard SMC
theory gives convergence as the number of particles increases. The paper should state this
formally for the proposed local-to-global construction.

5.3    Weight stability for variable selection
For variable selection, we can study when local proposals produce stable correction weights. A
possible direction is to show that, under weak cross-block correlation, the discrepancy between a
product of block posteriors and the full posterior is controlled by cross-block dependence terms.
   A possible informal statement is:




                                                5
Theorem 1 (Possible direction: weak cross-block dependence). Consider Gaussian linear re-
gression with a sparse prior on S. Suppose the design matrix can be partitioned into blocks
such that the cross-block correlations are uniformly small. Then, under suitable sparsity and
signal-strength conditions, the divergence between the local block proposal and the full poste-
rior is controlled by the magnitude of the cross-block covariance. Consequently, the variance of
importance weights remains bounded when cross-block dependence is sufficiently weak.

   The exact assumptions and proof are part of the student’s task. This theorem would for-
malize when local variable-selection proposals are useful.

5.4    Approximation error when exact correction is unavailable
If exact correction is computationally prohibitive, the paper should quantify approximation
error. Possible metrics include

ability error, and credible-interval coverage error.

6.1    Variable-selection simulations
Begin with Gaussian linear regression. Use settings such as

                 n ∈ {100, 200, 500},    p ∈ {100, 500, 1000},     s ∈ {5, 10, 20}.

Vary the following:

    1. within-block correlation;

    2. cross-block correlation;

    3. signal strength;

    4. block size;

    5. overlap size;

    6. prior sparsity.

    Compare:

    1. full posterior MCMC;

    2. naive split-and-merge;

    3. product of local posterior approximations;

    4. one-shot importance correction;

    5. tempered SMC correction;

    6. block-informed Metropolis–Hastings correction.

    Metrics:

    1. marginal posterior inclusion probability error;
    2. joint inclusion probability error;
    3. posterior model-size distribution;
    4. coefficient credible interval coverage;
    5. prediction interval coverage;
    6. effective sample size;

   The key demonstration should be a setting where naive split-and-merge screens variables
well but gives wrong posterior uncertainty, especially for correlated predictors placed in different

7     Development Plan
7.1    Phase 1: Literature review
The student should first write a literature memo organized by five themes:
    1. split-and-merge Bayesian variable selection;
    2. composite likelihood and approximate posterior calibration;
    3. divide-and-conquer Bayesian computation;
    4. importance tempering and SMC samplers;
    5. diagnostics for scalable Bayesian computation.
    The memo should answer:
    1. Which methods split data, variables, or model structures?
    2. Which methods target the exact posterior?
    3. Which methods target an approximation?
    4. Which methods give valid posterior uncertainty?
    5. Where is the gap for local-to-global structured model spaces?

7.2    Phase 2: Variable-selection prototype
The first implementation should be in Gaussian regression. The student should construct a
simple example where cross-block correlation causes naive merging to distort uncertainty. Then
the student should implement one correction method, preferably either Metropolis–Hastings
correction or a simple tempered SMC correction.

7.3    Phase 3: First theorem
The first theorem should be modest and clean. The best options are:
    1. exact posterior invariance under full-posterior Metropolis–Hastings correction;
    2. SMC consistency for a specified local-to-global target sequence;
    3. a weight-stability result under weak cross-block dependence in Gaussian regression.

7.4    Phase 5: Integrated paper
The paper should be written around the general principle and use variable selection as the main
application. The experiments should demonstrate across several dependence regimes that easy
merging is not enough for valid uncertainty.


8     Risks and Responses
8.1    Risk 1: The project is too broad
The framework could become too broad if it covers too many structured model spaces at once.
The response is to keep the general framework simple and use variable selection as the controlled
application:

                        variable selection: easy merge, hard uncertainty.

8.2    Risk 2: Novelty is challenged
A reviewer may say that the project combines known ideas: split-and-merge, SMC, and Metropolis–
Hastings. The response is that the contribution is a general posterior-correction framework for
local model-space exploration, with explicit attention to uncertainty distortion after merging.
    To make the novelty strong, the paper should include at least one new algorithmic device,
such as:

    1. adaptive local-to-global tempering;

    2. a proposal learned from local posterior samples;

    3. correction diagnostics based on effective sample size or local-global discrepancy;

    4. theory for stable correction weights.

8.3    Risk 3: Exact correction is expensive
The paper should separate exact and approximate variants. The exact variant uses full-posterior
Metropolis–Hastings or SMC targets ending at the full posterior. The approximate variant uses
local-to-global correction with quantified approximation error.

8.4    Risk 4: Local proposals miss important posterior regions
If local computations do not cover important global posterior regions, correction cannot recover
them well. The method should therefore use overlapping blocks, multiple random partitions,
adaptive blocking, tempering, rejuvenation, and diagnostics for degeneracy.


9     Why This Could Be a JASA or JRSSB Paper
A strong paper should make a broad statistical point:

       Local Bayesian computations are often used to scale posterior inference, but naive
       local-to-global merging can badly distort posterior uncertainty. We develop a cor-
       rection framework that uses local computations for speed while targeting or approx-
       imating the global posterior distribution.

    The paper should aim for three contributions:


                                                 8
  1. Conceptual contribution. Define posterior correction after local model-space explo-
     ration as a general Bayesian inference problem.

  2. Methodological contribution. Develop a divide–merge–correct Monte Carlo frame-
     work using SMC, tempering, and Metropolis–Hastings correction.

  3. Application contribution. Demonstrate the framework in Bayesian variable selection
     across a range of dependence structures and problem sizes.

   The ideal paper will have a clean algorithm, a rigorous validity result, one familiar statistical
example, and one high-impact scientific application.

12     Bottom Line
The project should aim to establish the following principle:


      Merging local Bayesian outputs is easy to do but hard to make statistically valid.

   Variable selection gives a controlled and familiar setting where the merge is easy but posterior
uncertainty can be wrong. It can support a broad methodology for local-to-global Bayesian
computation over structured model spaces while keeping the paper focused.


13     Other thoughts
