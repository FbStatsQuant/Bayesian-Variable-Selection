My intuitive read: v3 is a sound and careful method, and the right bet for v1. But it's less "local-to-global" and less parallel-native than its framing suggests. Here is what I think holds up and what worries me.

## What I like
- **The correction is the real idea, and it's right.** Correctness doesn't depend on the merge being good: SMC tempers to the exact posterior, and Ẑ is unbiased. That is precisely what Song–Liang lacks, and it's why correlation can't break us. I'm confident in this part.
- **The parallelism it has is real.** Islands are independent and need no communication, and running them at the same time is bit-for-bit identical to the serial run. Results can only get better as islands are added.
- **The study supports the "posterior accuracy first" framing.** Of the fits where the method missed the true model, 565 out of 2,400 were cases where the exact posterior itself puts its mode elsewhere at n = 200. Local search made 0 moves in 2,394 fits. So the sampler mostly finds what the posterior says. The limit is statistical, not computational.

## What worries me
1. **The block start ("local") step does less work than the name implies.** By the inventory's count, the block start holds the truth (the favourable regime) in about 1 of 2,394 study fits. Under masking, the start puts mass of order p⁻ᶜ on the true model. Almost all the work is done by the bridge and the moves, which is generic tempered SMC (Schäfer–Chopin 2013 already did parallel tempered SMC on binary spaces). A referee will ask: "what does the block start buy you over a plain independent-Bernoulli start?" I don't think we know. That's the one empirical gap I'd close before locking the framing.
2. **We parallelise over particles, not over variables.** Song–Liang's workers each hold n × p/K. Ours each need all of X (or XᵀX), and every move round steps through all p variables in sequence. So adding GPUs buys more particles, which improves accuracy. It does not reduce the per-particle cost as p grows, and memory per worker grows like p². For your long-term "retire MCMC at large p" vision, these are the bottlenecks. The fix would be a move kernel that scores all p flips at once, which is what GPUs are good at. I rejected that for v1 to protect the theorems and the study, and I still think that's right. But it should be the explicit headline of v2, not a footnote.
3. **Calibration is the weak point.** The pilot, up to ℓ rebuilds, the 4 diagnostic islands, the escalation tiers, the candidate set V and the frozen grid are each defensible. But calibration is:
   - the most sequential part (rebuilds must run one after another);
   - the least theorised part (nothing on rebuild convergence or stage count under masking);
   - the reason so many of our theorems hold only for an idealised version of the method.

   Biometrika referees like simple algorithms. I'd keep it, because changing it would invalidate the study, but present it as a schedule-construction step with a stated contract, not as part of the method's essence.
4. **The two PIP rules pull in opposite directions.** The equal-weight PIP, our primary, is what the study reports. But its error is set by the particle count per island, so adding islands doesn't fix it. The Ẑ-weighted PIP is the one that provably converges as islands are added, but it can be very noisy under masking. The parallel story and the reported numbers currently rest on different estimators. The paper needs to own that.
5. **There's a lot of machinery.** HPM as the best model ever scored, LS0, local search, the E1–E3 flags, resource guards. Each piece is there to make some theorem true, and the positive results about the flags are thin. Some of it may end up in the supplement.

## Bottom line
v3.1 is an honest, defensible v1: an SMC that targets the exact posterior, with parallel islands, unbiased evidence estimates and real theory. It beats split-and-merge on correctness under correlation. It is not yet the parallel-native sampler that retires MCMC at large p. That's a v2 kernel, plus scoring that doesn't need all of X on every worker.

## What I'd do now
Run a cheap ablation: v3 against the same SMC with a plain independent-Bernoulli start (no blocks), on about 10 designs × 2 seeds, at matched compute, on Owls.
- If the blocks clearly help (fewer stages, better Ẑ, better PIPs), the local-to-global framing is earned, and the "why the bridge" group of theorems carries weight.
- If they don't, the paper's story shifts to "exact-target parallel SMC with guarantees". It's still novel on theory, but the framing changes, and we'd want to know that before the theorem list is locked.

Noted; I'll wait for the rest before assembling the team. Here is my provisional answer to question 1 so it can shape the plan.

**Short answer: no, local-to-global doesn't hold back the parallel part.** It is the most parallel and cheapest part of the algorithm. What it changes is how much sequential work the rest needs, and that's what we have to measure.

- **The local step is embarrassingly parallel.** Each block's enumeration uses only that block's columns and y, and costs about J·2^B small scores, a small share of total work. It's the one stage that looks like Song–Liang's split step.
- **The sequential cost comes from SMC, not from local-to-global.** It comes from the bridge stages, the move rounds that step through all p variables, and the calibration rebuilds. A plain SMC without blocks would have the same sequential structure.
- **The one serial piece local-to-global adds is the clustering.** Complete linkage does p − 1 merges in sequence. That's negligible at study size and only matters at very large p. A faster exact linkage algorithm that keeps the same blocks is already a listed option (C11).
- **The start decides how many sequential stages SMC needs.** The proved stage laws say: O(1) stages when the start is favourable, and about log₂A₀ when it is masked, where A₀ measures how badly the start underweights the truth. So local-to-global's job is to reduce sequential depth, which puts it at the centre of the parallel story, not in its way.

**One correction to the framing:** the method doesn't run on composite likelihood. Only the start π₀ is composite; the target and every reported number come from the exact posterior. So the strong HPM results come from the exact correction plus the search, and we don't know yet how much the composite start contributes. The inventory counts the block start as favourable in about 1 of 2,394 study fits. That's why this can't be settled by argument alone.

**What would make dropping it irrefutable:** at matched total compute, across the study's p grid (low and high p, all correlation scenarios), plain SMC with no blocks would have to match v3.1 on:
- the number of stages (sequential depth);
- the variability of Ẑ across islands;
- PIP error;
- HPM accuracy;

and the blocks' setup cost would also have to grow faster in p than any saving. The test should include the backfit start as a third arm, since it was favourable in 79–92% of study fits.

Theory points the other way: depth grows with log A₀, so a better start cuts depth directly. My expectation is that local-to-global survives this test. If it doesn't, the comparison will make that case rather than any agent's opinion.

Noted, and saved: every component needs both a computational case and a proof route, and an unprovable heuristic counts for nothing. Here is my provisional view on the selection rule.

**My view: HPM stays primary, because it's the rule that is easiest to prove for an SMC output.** The reported model is the best model ever scored, compared on exact scores. So its error splits cleanly into two parts:

P(reported ≠ S₀) ≤ P(the exact posterior's mode ≠ S₀) + P(S₀ is never scored)

- The first term is pure statistics: T03♯ (Rossell 2022, Prop 1).
- The second term is a discovery statement: T38, N200, and the start-mode local search (LS0).
- Neither term needs the SMC weights to be accurate. That matters, because weight accuracy under masking is our thinnest theory.
- Computationally it's a max over the islands, the cheapest possible parallel reduction.
- I'd guess this is why it ranks so well across all scenarios: it is robust to the weight degeneracy that SMC suffers through masked crossings.

**Any rule built on PIPs inherits the PIP-accuracy problem.** That covers the median probability model, PIP thresholds, Bayesian FDR-type rules and credible sets. Each is provable for the exact posterior (Barbieri–Berger, Müller et al.). For our output it would also need a bound on PIP error, and in the masked regime that is our weakest area. Adding one as the primary rule would make the theory harder, which fails your criterion.

**What the team should still check, at almost no cost:** every saved fit already holds the scored set, the PIPs and Ẑ, so candidate rules can be compared after the fact on all 2,400 fits with no new runs. The candidates:
- MPM with equal-weight PIPs;
- MPM with Ẑ-weighted PIPs;
- HPM refined by local search, with a certificate (Theorem A);
- a hybrid that reports HPM and flags it when MPM disagrees.

A rule earns a place only if both hold:
1. It beats or complements HPM in identifiable scenarios. One example: designs where the exact posterior's mode misses S₀ (565 of the 2,400 fits), if MPM catches some of those.
2. Its error splits into a statistical term and a sampler term, each with a proof route.
